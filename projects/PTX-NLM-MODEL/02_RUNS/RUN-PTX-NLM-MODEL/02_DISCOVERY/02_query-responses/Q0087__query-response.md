[INFERRED] Clinical Design Implications:
Evaluation Windows: Assessing antifibrotic response at 3 or 4 months is premature. A minimum of 6 months is required to detect initial clinical response in systemic sclerosis, while 24 months is required to achieve peak benefit in radiation-induced fibrosis.
Optimal Duration: Short-term treatment courses (6–12 months) are insufficient for durable tissue remodeling. Optimal management of established fibrotic disease requires continuous, long-term combination therapy lasting at least 2 to 3+ years.
Withdrawal Strategy: Sudden discontinuation after short courses carries a high risk of relapse (+40%/yr). For patients achieving peak regression after 2 to 3 years, withdrawal should be accompanied by close clinical monitoring to manage minor long-term fibrotic progression (+8.5%/yr).
Synthesis and Key Implications
Evidence-Type Integration: Both trials supply human clinical evidence ([CLINICAL]). Proposed cellular mechanisms—such as phosphodiesterase inhibition downregulating TGF-β1 pathway signaling, suppressing fibroblast proliferation, reducing extracellular matrix deposition, and scavenging reactive oxygen species ([MECHANISM])—explain the observed tissue remodeling.
Duration-Dependent Efficacy & Relapse Protection: Short-term exposure (6 months) produces partial skin softening (8.4-point MRSS reduction in SSc; 46% surface reduction in RIF) but fails to induce complete remission and leaves patients vulnerable to rapid rebound (+40%/yr) upon drug cessation. Extended treatment (2 to 3+ years) maximizes tissue regression (68% surface reduction) and stabilizes the extracellular matrix against post-withdrawal relapse.
Remaining Evidentiary Gaps: While Delanian et al. (2005) provide robust long-term off-drug washout data in radiation fibrosis, De Souza et al. (2009) lacked a post-treatment follow-up phase. Randomized, double-blind trials with mandatory off-drug monitoring are needed to confirm post-withdrawal stability in systemic sclerosis.
