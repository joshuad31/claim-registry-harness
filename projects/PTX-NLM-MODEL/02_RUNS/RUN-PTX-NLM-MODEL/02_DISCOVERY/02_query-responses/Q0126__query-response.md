A. Koo et al. (2000) (koo2000.pdf.md)
[PRECLINICAL] Model & Species: Male Sprague-Dawley rats (275–325 g) were subjected to polymicrobial sepsis via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) with subcutaneous saline fluid resuscitation (3 mL/100 g body weight) (koo2000.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS - Animal model of polymicrobial sepsis).
[PRECLINICAL] Dose, Route, and Timing: Initiated 1 hour post-CLP, rats received a single continuous IV femoral infusion of 50 mg/kg body weight PTX in 1 mL normal saline over 90 minutes (koo2000.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS - Administration of PTX). Vehicle controls received 1 mL normal saline IV over 90 minutes (koo2000.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS - Administration of PTX). All endpoints were assayed 20 hours post-CLP (the late, hypodynamic stage of sepsis) (koo2000.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
B. Wu et al. (1999) (wu1999.pdf.md)
[PRECLINICAL] Conscious Mouse Endotoxemic Survival Model: Male conscious mice were subjected to severe endotoxemia via intraperitoneal injection of E. coli lipopolysaccharide (LPS 60 mg/kg IP) (wu1999.pdf.md, 3.5; Fig 5).
[PRECLINICAL] Dose, Route, and Timing: PTX was administered therapeutically as an IP injection of 3 mg/kg IP given at 0, 6, 15, and 24 hours post-LPS injection (cumulative dose: 12 mg/kg) (wu1999.pdf.md, 3.5. Effects of pentoxifylline on the survival rate of mice treated with lipopolysaccharide).
[PRECLINICAL] Anesthetized Rat Endotoxemia Model: Male Wistar-Kyoto rats received E. coli LPS (10 mg/kg IV). PTX was administered as a single 3 mg/kg IV dose 30 minutes prior to LPS (wu1999.pdf.md, Abstract; 3.1).
C. Waxman et al. (1991) (waxman1991.pdf.md)
[PRECLINICAL] Model & Species: Male Sprague-Dawley rats (300–350 g) were subjected to fixed-volume arterial phlebotomy (21 mL/kg over 5 minutes), producing severe hemorrhagic shock (mean arterial pressure [MAP] ~35 mmHg) (waxman1991.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
[PRECLINICAL] Dose, Route, and Timing: Following a 15-minute hypotensive delay, fluid resuscitation with Lactated Ringer's solution (2× shed blood volume over 45 min) was initiated. PTX was added to the resuscitation fluid as a 25 mg/kg IV bolus followed immediately by a 25 mg/kg IV infusion over 45 minutes (total dose: 50 mg/kg IV) (waxman1991.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS). Placebo animals received an equivalent volume of normal saline IV (waxman1991.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).

Early Resuscitation Adjunct (Low-to-Moderate Dose, Slow Infusion): Administering PTX as a 25 mg/kg bolus + 25 mg/kg 45-min infusion concurrently with fluid resuscitation starting 15 to 60 minutes post-insult (Waxman 1991, Koo 2000) provides maximal physiological benefit. Fractionated Low-Dose Post-Injury Therapy: Administering low individual doses (3 mg/kg IP) on a repeated schedule (0, 6, 15, and 24 h post-insult) avoids peak-dose systemic vasodilation while providing continuous TNF-α and iNOS suppression, producing a 3.5-fold increase in 36-hour survival (35% vs 10%, P<0.05) (wu1999.pdf.md, 3.5; Fig 5).
Patterns Producing Hemodynamic Harm or Mortality:
High Single Bolus / Double-Dose Infusion in Established Shock: Waxman et al. and Wu et al. cite foundational literature demonstrating that administering high single boluses (≥50 mg/kg) or delayed infusions after septic/hemorrhagic shock is fully established induces uncontrolled, fatal systemic arterial hypotension and increases early mortality (waxman1991.pdf.md, Discussion; barroso-aranda1990.pdf.md, Discussion).
Unresuscitated Vasodilation: Administering PTX without aggressive fluid volume replacement exacerbates hypovolemic hypotension due to peripheral venodilation and loss of compensatory vascular tone (waxman1991.pdf.md, Discussion).

Key Implications for Practice:
Post-injury PTX is highly effective as a resuscitation adjunct when delivered early (within 1 hour) using slow infusion or fractionated low-dose protocols combined with fluid expansion.
Delayed high-dose bolus administration in unresuscitated shock must be avoided due to the risk of precipitating fatal vasodilatory collapse.
