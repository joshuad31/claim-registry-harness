1. Masterson et al. 2017 (masterson2017.pdf.md)
[CLINICAL] Prescribed Treatment Exposure: 14 men with idiopathic chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS) were enrolled to undergo a 10-visit comprehensive PFPT program (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods).
[CLINICAL] Attendance & Completion: 10 of 14 men (71.4%) completed all 10 visits (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results). The remaining 4 men (28.6%) completed between 5 and 9 visits and were actively in the process of completing their 10 sessions at data cutoff (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results).
[CLINICAL] Abandonment for Perceived Inefficacy: 0 patients (0%) abandoned therapy due to lack of efficacy or symptom exacerbation; no patient demonstrated an increase in GUPI score (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results, Figure 1).
[CLINICAL] Home Adherence: Home exercise compliance was not tracked or reported independently of clinical attendance (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods).
[CLINICAL] Completed-Treatment Nonresponse: Of the 10 men completing 10 visits, 3 patients (30%) were nonresponders (GUPI total score reduction <4 points) (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results). Moderate response (GUPI reduction 4–6 points) occurred in 2 patients (20%), and robust response (GUPI reduction ≥7 points) occurred in 5 patients (50%) (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results).
2. Anderson et al. 2011 (anderson2011.pdf.md)
[CLINICAL] Prescribed Treatment Exposure: 157 enrolled patients (93% male; median age 41 years) completed a 6-day clinic immersion program and were prescribed home self-treatment using an internal pelvic therapeutic wand (3–4 times/week, 5–10 min/session for 6 months) alongside daily external muscle massage, stretching, and progressive relaxation (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS, RESULTS).
[CLINICAL] Attendance & Completion: 113 of 157 patients (72.0%; 106 men and 7 women) completed 6 months of wand use (anderson2011.pdf.md, RESULTS, TABLE 2).
[CLINICAL] Categorized Abandonment & Withdrawal Counts (44/157 Total, 28.0%):
Abandonment for Perceived Inefficacy: 3 patients (1.9%) stopped because the wand was "not effective for pain relief" (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Protocol Noncompliance: 10 patients (6.4%) failed to use the wand at least once weekly (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Lost to Follow-up: 10 patients (6.4%) failed to complete questionnaires (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Pre-dispensing / External Non-use: 12 patients (7.6%) did not use the wand after dispensing (4 never used—reason unspecified; 6 precluded by unrelated illness/injury; 2 sought external physical therapy) (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Symptom Resolution: 5 patients (3.2%) stopped because pain/trigger point sensitivity resolved and they no longer needed the wand (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Alternative Self-Treatment: 5 patients (3.2%) preferred internal massage with a finger (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Anatomic / Tissue Intolerance: 5 patients (3.2%) withdrew due to a hypersensitive pelvic floor (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Device / Technique Difficulty: 6 patients (3.8%) withdrew due to intimidation/clumsiness (3), technical algometer issues (2), or difficulty locating trigger points (1) (anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
[CLINICAL] Adverse Event Discontinuations: 0 serious adverse events; 0 patients (0%) discontinued therapy due to adverse events (anderson2011.pdf.md, Safety Assessments). Minor transient events (anal soreness, trace urethral/prostatic bleeding) resolved without medical intervention or treatment interruption (anderson2011.pdf.md, Safety Assessments).
[CLINICAL] Completed-Treatment Nonresponse: Among 109 evaluable patients at 6 months, 14 patients (12.8%) experienced no reduction in trigger point sensitivity (VAS change ≤0) (anderson2011.pdf.md, Pelvic Muscle TrP Sensitivity, Figure 3). Overall, 5 of 111 patients (4.5%) rated the wand as "not effective" (anderson2011.pdf.md, Pelvic Muscle TrP Sensitivity).
Intervention Components
[CLINICAL] Masterson et al. 2017: Comprehensive 4-modality protocol administered by specialized physical therapists:
Manual Therapy: Internal and external myofascial trigger point (TrP) release of pelvic floor and abdominal musculature (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods).
Therapeutic Exercise: Range-of-motion, mobility, flexibility, and strengthening of weak muscles (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods).
Biofeedback: Visualizing pelvic floor muscle activity to facilitate strengthening and relaxation (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods).
Neuromodulation: Electrical stimulation for pelvic floor muscle relaxation and pain relief (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods).
[CLINICAL] Anderson et al. 2011: Multimodal self-treatment protocol:
6-Day Intensive Clinic Immersion: Individualized mapping of tender internal/external TrPs and education (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
Internal Therapeutic Wand: Shepherd's crook Ultem plastic wand with an integrated strain-gauge algometer for rectal self-release of internal TrPs (3–4 times/week, 5–10 min/session, torque pressure ≤0.34 kg/cm2) (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
Home Physical Techniques: External muscle massage and stretching (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
Progressive Relaxation: Daily home paradoxical relaxation training (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
Male Urinary Outcomes versus Pain Outcomes
[CLINICAL] Masterson et al. 2017: Pain and global symptoms were measured using the composite Genitourinary Pain Index (GUPI; 0–45 scale), which decreased from a median of 30.8 [16–39] at baseline to 22.2 [7–37] at visit 10 (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results).
[NO SOURCE] Isolated Male Voiding Parameters: Neither masterson2017.pdf.md nor anderson2011.pdf.md measured or reported isolated male micturition endpoints, such as maximum flow rate (Qmax), post-void residual (PVR) volume, International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), or voiding diary frequency.
[CLINICAL] Anderson et al. 2011: Evaluated myofascial pain sensitivity using a 10-point VAS. Median trigger point sensitivity decreased from 7.5 (IQR 6.0–8.5) at baseline to 5.0 (IQR 4.0–6.5) at 1 month and 4.0 (IQR 2.5–5.5) at 6 months (P<0.001) (anderson2011.pdf.md, Pelvic Muscle TrP Sensitivity, Figure 3). 95.5% rated the wand as very or moderately effective for pain relief (anderson2011.pdf.md, Pelvic Muscle TrP Sensitivity).
Cointerventions and Selection Limitations
[CLINICAL] Masterson et al. 2017:
Cointerventions: Uncontrolled combination of four simultaneous physical therapy modalities (masterson2017.pdf.md, Methods). Patients had previously tried antibiotics, alpha-blockers, and anti-inflammatory medications (masterson2017.pdf.md, Introduction).
Selection Limitations: Small pilot sample (n=14), single tertiary center, open-label design lacking a control group, self-selected referral population, and variable treatment spacing (time to 10th visit ranged from 44 to 192 days due to insurance and availability) (masterson2017.pdf.md, Table 1, Discussion).
[CLINICAL] Anderson et al. 2011:
Cointerventions: Wand self-treatment was co-administered with external muscle massage, stretching, paradoxical relaxation exercises, and ongoing prescription/OTC medications maintained without modification (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS).
Selection Limitations: Highly self-selected, highly motivated patient cohort who paid out-of-pocket to attend a 6-day immersion clinic after failing traditional medical care (anderson2011.pdf.md, MATERIALS AND METHODS, Discussion). Lacked a sham control group, relied on telephone/questionnaire follow-up due to distant patient geographic distribution, and had a heavy male predominance (93%) (anderson2011.pdf.md, Discussion).
Defining Prior-Treatment History in a Future Voiding Trial
To prevent confounding in a future male voiding or LUTS trial, prior-treatment history must be defined by objective criteria rather than vague self-reports (masterson2017.pdf.md, Introduction; anderson2011.pdf.md, Discussion):
[INFERRED] Verified Pharmacotherapy Exposure: Mandate documentation of specific drug classes, verified daily doses, and minimum treatment durations (e.g., ≥8 weeks of alpha-blockers, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors, or anticholinergics) (masterson2017.pdf.md, Introduction).
[INFERRED] Separation of Pharmacotherapy vs. Physical Therapy Failure: Distinguish failure of systemic drugs from prior exposure to pelvic floor interventions (masterson2017.pdf.md, Introduction; anderson2011.pdf.md, Discussion).
[INFERRED] Phenotyping Prior Physical Therapy Modalities: Differentiate specialized internal/external myofascial trigger point release and relaxation biofeedback from inappropriate pelvic floor strengthening (e.g., Kegel exercises), which Masterson et al. note can exacerbate hypertonic pelvic floor muscle spasm and worsen voiding symptoms in CPPS (masterson2017.pdf.md, Discussion).
[INFERRED] Categorization of Discontinuation Reasons: Classify prior treatment termination into true pharmacodynamic/neuromuscular nonresponse, adverse event intolerance, financial/insurance exhaustion, or lack of provider specialization (masterson2017.pdf.md, Discussion; anderson2011.pdf.md, TABLE 2).
Synthesis and Key Implications
Evidence Types:
Clinical Evidence: Demonstrates high completion rates (∼71–72%) and low perceived inefficacy abandonment (≤1.9%) in prospective male CPPS/UCPPS cohorts undergoing specialized multimodal physical therapy or wand self-treatment (masterson2017.pdf.md, Results; anderson2011.pdf.md, RESULTS, TABLE 2).
